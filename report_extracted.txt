1. Project Overview 
Project Title: Anna-Setu - "Anna" (food/sustenance) + "Setu" (bridge) - the AI-powered bridge between campus 
surplus and community need. 
Primary SDG Alignment: SDG 12 - Responsible Consumption and Production. The project directly targets Target 
12.3, which calls for halving per-capita global food waste at the retail and consumer level by 2030. 
Secondary SDG Alignment: SDG 2 - Zero Hunger. Rescued surplus is redirected to food-insecure individuals and 
community shelters, converting an institutional inefficiency into a source of local nutrition security. 
Anna-Setu is a conceptual AI system for higher -education and school campuses that pairs predictive demand 
forecasting with an automated, policy-compliant surplus-matching pipeline turning cafeteria over-production from 
an environmental liability into a measurable social good. 
2. Problem Statement 
“How might we use AI to accurately forecast daily meal demand and intelligently match unavoidable 
surplus food with verified local shelters, so that campus dining ecosystems and food-insecure communities 
can become more sustainable?” 
Contextualising the Problem 
Most university and school mess halls and cafeterias operate on fixed, historical menu-planning cycles rather than 
on real, day-specific demand signals. This creates a structural mismatch between food prepared and food consumed: 
• Overproduction as a default safety margin: Kitchen staff routinely over -cook by 10 –30% to avoid the 
reputational and logistical cost of running out, with no data-driven basis for the margin chosen. 
• No demand forecasting: Menus are planned weeks in advance on a rotating cycle, blind to variables that swing 
actual footfall, mid-semester and end-semester exam weeks, festival and holiday clusters, weather (heavy rain 
or extreme heat reduces walk-in attendance), and one-off campus events. 
• Fragmented, manual surplus handling: When surplus does exist, there is typically no standing relationship, 
verified contact list, or safety -checked handoff process with a local shelter or food bank , so left-over food is 
either held unsafely, distributed ad hoc, or simply discarded. 
• Waste ends in landfill, not redistribution: Without a forecasting-plus-matching system, the default outcome for 
surplus is disposal, which generates methane during anaerobic decomposition and wastes the water, energy, 
and labour embedded in producing that food in the first place. 
• No feedback loop: Cafeteria managers rarely have visibility into how their over/under -production decisions 
compare to actual consumption over time, so the same inefficiencies repeat semester after semester. 
These four gaps - no forecasting, no formal matching mechanism, no compliance layer, and no feedback loop - are 
precisely what an AI-driven system is positioned to close. 
3. Detailed Solution Description (Design Thinking Approach) 
Target Users 

=== PAGE 3 ===

• Campus students and diners - as both demand -signal generators (via meal swipes, app RSVPs) and, where 
relevant, beneficiaries of reduced mess fees from cost savings. 
• Cafeteria/mess management and kitchen staff - the primary decision-makers who plan procurement, cooking 
quantities, and staffing. 
• Local food shelter coordinators and NGO partners - the receiving end of the redistribution pipeline, who need 
reliable, safe, and timely surplus notifications. 
• Campus sustainability/administration office - who track SDG-linked impact metrics for institutional ESG and 
accreditation reporting. 
Empathize & Define - Current Operational Gaps 
• Kitchen staff have no simple, low -effort way to log daily consumption versus preparation quantities, so 
historical data (if it exists at all) is inconsistent or paper-based. 
• Mess managers lack visibility into upcoming campus events, exam calendars, or weather forecasts at the point 
of menu and quantity planning. 
• There is no trusted, pre -vetted directory of shelters willing and able to receive surplus at short notice, nor a 
standard protocol for what is safe to donate. 
• Students and diners have no channel to signal attendance intent in advance (e.g., “skipping dinner tonight”), 
so demand remains a guess rather than a forecast. 
• Sustainability data - waste weight, cost of over-production, meals redirected - is not centrally tracked, so the 
institution cannot demonstrate or improve its SDG 12 performance. 
Ideation - The Solution: Smart Campus Dining Advisor & Predictive Redistribution Pipeline 
Anna-Setu is built around two connected AI-driven modules: 
Module 1 - Smart Campus Dining Advisor: A predictive analytics layer that forecasts next-day (and next-meal) 
attendance and consumption per menu item, using historical mess logs, the academic calendar (exams, holidays, 
events), and weather data. It issues a recommended cooking quantity to kitche n staff each morning, with a stated 
confidence range, replacing the current “cook for a fixed number, hope for the best” approach. 
Module 2 - Predictive Redistribution Pipeline: An automated matching and compliance layer that activates when 
the Advisor detects (or kitchen staff report) a surplus above a safe-to-donate threshold. It checks the surplus against 
food-safety and holding-time rules, identifies the nearest available and verified shelter partner from a maintained 
directory, and drafts and sends a structured surplus alert, by text or WhatsApp-style message, so a pickup or drop-
off can be arranged within a safe time window. 
Together, the two modules shift the campus dining operation from reactive waste disposal to proactive waste 
prevention plus responsible redistribution of whatever surplus remains unavoidable. 
Expected Impact 
Dimension Expected Impact 
Economic Reduced cafeteria food-procurement costs through demand-matched cooking (targeted 15–25% 
reduction in over-production); lower institutional waste-disposal fees; potential CSR/ESG 
recognition for the campus.

4. AI Elements, Tools, & Logical Workflow 
Core AI Components 
Predictive Analytics / Machine Learning: A time-series and regression-based forecasting model (e.g., gradient-
boosted trees or a simple LSTM) trained on historical meal-log data, day-of-week and academic-calendar features 
(exam periods, holidays, orientation weeks), and external weather data. Output: a per-meal, per-menu-item demand 
forecast with a confidence interval, refreshed daily. 
IBM Granite Models & Prompt Engineering: An IBM Granite foundation model, driven by structured prompt 
templates, powers the natural-language layer of the system - turning structured surplus data (quantity, item, safe -
until time, pickup location) into a clear, actionable WhatsApp/SMS -style broadcast message for shelter 
coordinators, and answering kitchen-staff questions about the day's forecast in plain language. 
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): A RAG pipeline indexes local municipal food -safety guidelines, 
FSSAI (or equivalent regional) food -handling and donation -compliance documents, and the institution's own 
donation policy. Before any surplus alert is generated, the system retrieves the releva nt compliance clauses so the 
drafted message and the underlying recommendation stay grounded in actual, current regulation rather than the 
model's unguided assumptions. 
Step-by-Step Logical Workflow 
Step Stage Description 
1 Data Collection Daily meal-swipe counts, kitchen preparation quantities, and plate-waste logs are 
recorded (manually via a simple form, or automatically via POS/turnstile data). 
Academic-calendar events and next-day weather are pulled in automatically. 
2 Demand Forecasting The ML model processes the combined dataset each evening and produces the next 
day's recommended cooking quantity per menu item, with a confidence range and a 
plain-language explanation of the key drivers. 
3 Kitchen Decision 
Support 
Mess managers view the forecast on a simple dashboard, can accept or manually 
adjust it, and place their procurement/cooking order for the next day — reducing 
the default over-production margin. 
4 Real-Time Surplus 
Detection 
During and after service, the system compares actual consumption against quantity 
prepared. If leftover food exceeds a configurable safe-to-donate threshold, it flags a 
surplus event. 
5 Compliance Check 
(RAG) 
The RAG pipeline retrieves the applicable food-safety and holding-time rules for 
the flagged food category and confirms the surplus is within a safe donation 
window before proceeding. 

=== PAGE 5 ===

6 Shelter Matching The system checks a maintained directory of verified local shelters/NGOs for 
availability, proximity, and capacity, and selects the best-matched recipient(s) for 
the quantity and type of surplus. 
7 AI-Drafted Alert 
(Granite + Prompting) 
The IBM Granite model, using the prompt template in Section 5, converts the 
structured surplus and compliance data into a clear, human-readable 
WhatsApp/SMS broadcast to the matched shelter coordinator(s). 
8 Human-in-the-Loop 
Confirmation 
A designated kitchen/administration staff member reviews and approves the drafted 
alert before it is sent, keeping a human decision-maker in control of the final 
action. 
9 Pickup / Redistribution The shelter coordinator confirms and arranges pickup or drop-off within the 
specified safe window; the handoff is logged for traceability. 
10 Feedback & Reporting Actual outcomes (quantity redistributed, forecast accuracy, cost saved, estimated 
methane avoided) are logged and fed back into the model for continuous 
improvement, and rolled up into a sustainability dashboard for the campus. 
5. Prototype Logic & Prompt Samples 
The example below shows a system prompt and structured input that would drive an LLM (e.g., an IBM Granite 
model) to automatically draft a compliant, ready -to-send “Surplus Food Alert” broadcast once Module 2 has 
confirmed a safe, matched surplus event. 
System Prompt 
SYSTEM PROMPT: 
You are the messaging assistant for Anna-Setu, a campus food-redistribution 
system. Your only task is to convert structured surplus-food data into a 
short, clear, and warm broadcast message for a verified shelter coordinator.  
 
Rules you must always follow: 
1. Use only the facts provided in the input data. Never invent quantities,  
   times, food items, or locations. 
2. Always state the safe-pickup-by time exactly as given. 
3. Reference the retrieved compliance note in one short line so the  
   coordinator knows the food has been checked against safety guidelines.  
4. Keep the message under 60 words, in plain, respectful language suitable  
   for WhatsApp/SMS. 
5. End with a clear call to action (how to confirm pickup). 
6. Do not include any student-identifying or personal data. 
Structured Input (Retrieved + Detected Data) 
{ 
  "surplus_item": "Vegetable pulao and dal", 
  "quantity_servings": 45,

Hi Asha Community Shelter team, we have 45 servings of vegetable pulao

6. Responsible AI Considerations (Mandatory) 
Anna-Setu is designed with responsible AI as a first-class requirement, not an afterthought. The table below maps 
each mandated pillar to a concrete design decision. 
Pillar Focus How Anna-Setu Addresses It 
Fairness Avoid biased 
assumptions across 
demographics, hostel 
blocks, or meal 
preferences 
Forecasting models are trained on pooled, de-identified consumption logs 
rather than individually profiled students; demand predictions are validated 
across dietary categories (vegetarian, non-vegetarian, Jain, vegan) and 
residential blocks to ensure no group is systematically under- or over-
served. Model outputs are periodically audited against actual consumption 
to catch drift or skew. 
Transparency Explainable predictions 
for cafeteria managers 
and administrators 
Every demand forecast is accompanied by a plain-language rationale (e.g., 
'Predicted 12% lower footfall - mid-semester exams begin tomorrow and 
historical logs show a similar dip'). Dashboards expose the top three factors 
driving each prediction so non-technical mess staff can question or override 
recommendations. 
Ethics Strict food safety and 
compliance adherence 
The system will never recommend redistribution of food that has exceeded 
safe holding-temperature windows or hygiene thresholds. All redistribution 
suggestions are gated by a rules layer that checks preparation time, storage 
conditions, and locally applicable food-safety guidelines before any alert is 
generated. 

=== PAGE 7 ===

Privacy Protecting student data 
anonymity 
Meal-swipe and consumption data are aggregated at the cohort level (e.g., 
'Block C dinner turnout') and stripped of individual identifiers before being 
used for forecasting. No student-level eating pattern, health condition, or 
dietary restriction is ever shared with external shelter partners or stored 
beyond the academic term.
